\begin{abstract}
Learned vulnerability detectors generalise poorly across projects, and the
field has largely responded with new architectures. We ask instead how much of
the gap is attributable to the node representation, and find that the answer is
both larger than the architecture effect and simpler than expected. Holding
graph topology, backbone, depth, readout and training budget fixed, and varying
only what a node carries, we compare project-specific lexical tokens against
three alternatives: a hand-designed taxonomy of operation types built from API
allow-lists and identifier rules, the parser's own grammar node kinds, which
are free, and their combination. Under project-level GroupKFold with
deduplication, mega-projects held train-only, and cluster-bootstrap intervals
over held-out projects, the two representations that contain grammar kinds beat
lexical tokens in all eight comparisons across two corpora and three
message-passing schemes ($+0.049$ to $+0.106$ AUC, seven of eight intervals
excluding zero), while the same architectures under a fixed representation
differ by roughly a third as much. The engineered taxonomy is not what produces
this. On its own it is indistinguishable from lexical features
($p \approx 0.9$); adding it to grammar kinds gives no consistent gain; and
refining it from eight to thirty-two operation types, on matched folds, changes
nothing. We explain these null results rather than merely reporting them. An
exact information-theoretic analysis shows the taxonomy is $98.2\%$ predictable
from the grammar kind ($H(\phi \mid \kappa) = 0.06$ bits), so it adds almost
nothing the parser has not already supplied, while discarding $69\%$ of the
kind's information ($H(\kappa \mid \phi) = 3.12$ bits), which is why it is weak
alone. We also validate the taxonomy as a static analysis against annotated
ground truth ($0.976$ call-site-weighted accuracy), which exposed three defects
invisible to downstream accuracy. The practical implication is a one-line
change --- substitute grammar kinds for lexical tokens --- and a methodological
one: an engineered abstraction over a parse tree should be checked for
redundancy against the grammar alphabet, in both directions, before it is
credited with an effect.
\end{abstract}
