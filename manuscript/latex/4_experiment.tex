\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiment}
\label{sec:results}

We design the evaluation to answer five research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Cross-Project Generalisation):} How does VulMorph-Fed compare to centralised and federated baselines --- including baselines that share its backbone or its input representation --- on held-out projects never seen by any client?
    \item \textbf{RQ1b (Value of Federation):} What do private federated clients add on top of a model trained centrally on public data alone?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Ablations):} What do VCSA, MCFPA and MGMP contribute individually, and how sensitive is the framework to the taxonomy size $|\mathcal{T}|$?
    \item \textbf{RQ3 (Privacy-Utility):} How does detection quality degrade across per-round DP budgets $\varepsilon$, accounting for composition over rounds?
    \item \textbf{RQ4 (Scalability):} How do performance and communication cost behave as the number of clients $K$ grows?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\subsubsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}

We evaluate on four public C/C++ vulnerability corpora with complementary characteristics:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Devign}~\cite{zhou2019devign}: 21{,}854 manually labelled functions from FFmpeg and QEMU ($\approx$46\% vulnerable); no per-function CWE annotations.
    \item \textbf{BigVul}~\cite{fan2020bigvul}: CVE-linked functions from 234 projects (in our sample), with CWE annotations; realistic class imbalance ($\approx$6\% vulnerable).
    \item \textbf{DiverseVul}~\cite{chen2023diversevul}: the most project-diverse corpus (580 projects in our sample, 59 CWE types; $\approx$5.5\% vulnerable).
    \item \textbf{PrimeVul}~\cite{ding2025primevul}: the strictest recent benchmark, with rigorous de-duplication and label verification.
\end{enumerate}
Devign is loaded from its public HuggingFace mirror, BigVul and DiverseVul from the \texttt{bstee615} mirrors, and PrimeVul from the \texttt{ASSERT-KTH} mirror (unpaired training split); exact dataset revisions are pinned in the released code. For computational tractability every experiment uses the first 8{,}000 functions of each corpus (after discarding functions shorter than three tokens); we report this cap explicitly since sampling affects absolute numbers.

\subsubsection{Cross-project federated split}
\label{sec:split}

All splits are at \emph{project} granularity: every function of a repository is assigned to exactly one partition. Projects are sorted by sample count and the smallest projects are held out until they total $\approx$20\% of samples --- these held-out projects form the test set, and \emph{no function from any test project is ever seen by any client}. The remaining training projects are assigned round-robin (by project) to $K = 4$ clients, so each client holds a disjoint set of repositories, simulating per-organisation codebases. For BigVul, DiverseVul and PrimeVul this yields hundreds of held-out test projects; for Devign, which contains only two projects, the split degenerates to training on one project and testing on the other (a pure cross-project transfer task). Every experiment is repeated with three random seeds $\{42, 43, 44\}$, which control shuffling, client assignment, model initialisation, and DP noise; we report mean $\pm$ standard deviation throughout, and per-seed values feed the statistical tests below.

\subsubsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines_setup}

The baseline suite is constructed to \emph{isolate} the two factors that could otherwise confound the comparison --- the input representation and the aggregation mechanism:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Centralised GGNN / GAT (lexical graphs)}: Devign-style~\cite{zhou2019devign} and CPVD-style~\cite{zhang2023cpvd} backbones trained on pooled raw data with full lexical token features --- the standard non-private reference points.
    \item \textbf{Centralised Transformer (sequence)}: a Transformer encoder over raw token sequences (structure-free sequence-model family, LineVul-style~\cite{fu2022linevul}). We note explicitly that this is a compact model trained from scratch, not a pre-trained code LM; pre-trained LM baselines require GPU budgets outside this study's scope and are left to future work.
    \item \textbf{Centralised GGNN (VCSA graphs)}: the GGNN backbone consuming our abstracted representation --- isolating the contribution of the representation from that of the FL protocol.
    \item \textbf{FedAvg + GAT (lexical graphs)}: the standard federated baseline.
    \item \textbf{FedAvg + GGNN (VCSA graphs)}: FedAvg with the \emph{same} input representation as VulMorph-Fed --- so any difference to our method is attributable to the aggregation mechanism (parameters vs.\ DP prototypes) and MGMP, not the representation.
    \item \textbf{FedAvg + Transformer (sequence)}: federated sequence-model baseline.
    \item \textbf{Centralised VulMorph (oracle)}: our full model on pooled data without federation or DP --- the upper reference for our own architecture.
\end{itemize}
All baselines use the same data, splits, seeds, class-weighted loss, optimiser and training budget as VulMorph-Fed; FedAvg aggregation is sample-size weighted.

\subsubsection{Implementation details}

All models are implemented in PyTorch~2.10 / PyTorch Geometric~2.7 and trained on CPU. VulMorph-Fed uses a 2-layer GNN, hidden dimension $d = 128$, embedding dimension 64, dropout 0.3, Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size 64, $T = 10$ federated rounds with $E = 2$ local epochs, $|\mathcal{C}| = 10$ CWE buckets, taxonomy size $|\mathcal{T}| = 8$, per-round budget $\varepsilon = 2.0$ and clip radius $R = 1$ unless stated otherwise. Inference follows the ensemble protocol of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ensemble}; the decision threshold is fixed at 0.5 for all systems (no test-set threshold tuning).

\subsubsection{Statistical testing}

For each baseline we pair the per-dataset, per-seed F1 values of VulMorph-Fed with those of the baseline (up to $4 \times 3 = 12$ pairs) and run a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Cliff's $\delta$ effect size; we mark a comparison significant at $p < 0.05$. With three seeds per dataset the per-dataset tests are underpowered on their own, which is why we test across the pooled dataset--seed pairs and additionally release all raw per-seed values in the artifact.

\subsubsection{Reproducibility}

The complete implementation --- loaders, models, federated runners, baselines, and the scripts that regenerate every table and figure in this paper from scratch (\texttt{experiments/run\_all.sh}) --- is publicly available.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/vinhqdang/vulmorph-fed}} Every table in this paper is generated programmatically from the versioned result files; no number is entered by hand.

\subsection{RQ1: Cross-Project Generalisation}

Table~\ref{tab:rq1_main} and Figure~\ref{fig:rq1_performance} report cross-project F1 on the four corpora. Three observations stand out.

First, VulMorph-Fed outperforms every privacy-preserving (federated) baseline on every dataset, including \emph{FedAvg + GGNN on the same VCSA representation}. Since that baseline shares our input representation, backbone family, data, and budget, the margin over it is attributable to the prototype-based aggregation and MGMP rather than to the representation --- directly addressing the representation/mechanism confound.

Second, comparing the two centralised GGNN rows (lexical vs.\ VCSA input) quantifies the contribution of the representation alone under pooled data, while comparing the two FedAvg graph variants quantifies it under federation. The abstraction is most valuable precisely in the federated cross-project setting, consistent with our motivating claim: lexical features do not transfer across project boundaries, so removing them costs little generalisation while removing the main source of client drift.

Third, absolute cross-project F1 remains far from the within-project levels reported in the older literature --- consistent with the replication studies~\cite{chakraborty2022deep, chakraborty2024realvul, ding2025primevul}. We deliberately do not claim to have ``closed'' the generalisation gap; we claim, and the data supports, a consistent improvement over comparable baselines under matched conditions. The pooled Wilcoxon tests against each baseline are released in \texttt{results/significance.json}; comparisons that do not reach $p < 0.05$ are stated as such rather than described as significant.

\subsection{RQ1b: What Does Federation Add Beyond Public Data?}

A fair question is whether federation is needed at all when large public vulnerability corpora exist: if morphologies are project-invariant, a centralised model on public data might transfer to private codebases without any federation. Table~\ref{tab:rq1b_public} addresses this directly. We train (A) the full VulMorph model centrally on a ``public'' half of the training data, and (B) the same public partition joined by private clients holding the other half, participating only through DP prototype federation. Both are evaluated on the same held-out projects. The delta between (B) and (A) is the measured value of private participation: private clients contribute CWE-conditioned structural patterns whose prevalence or variants are under-represented in the public partition, without revealing code. We report this delta with its variance rather than asserting it; where it is small, that is itself an informative bound on the federation argument, and we discuss the implications honestly in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{RQ2: Ablations and Taxonomy Sensitivity}

Table~\ref{tab:rq2_ablation} removes one component at a time on Devign. Removing the morphological abstraction (reverting to lexical node features) and removing VCSA's learned edge weighting both degrade cross-project F1, confirming that the representation carries a large share of the transfer signal. Replacing MCFPA's affinity weighting with a uniform average, or MGMP with plain message passing, yields intermediate drops. ``Local only'' (no federation) exposes the net value of federation under DP, and the ``w/o DP'' row quantifies the price of privacy at the default budget.

Table~\ref{tab:rq2b_taxonomy} varies the taxonomy size $|\mathcal{T}| \in \{8, 16, 32\}$ using strictly nested refinements of the same rule set, addressing the concern that $|\mathcal{T}| = 8$ may be too coarse to separate vulnerability classes. Differences across taxonomy sizes are reported with seed variance so the reader can judge whether granularity matters within the studied range; we adopt $|\mathcal{T}| = 8$ as the default because larger taxonomies increase the feature dimensionality without a corresponding gain in our experiments.

\subsection{RQ3: Privacy-Utility Trade-off}

Table~\ref{tab:rq3_privacy} and Figure~\ref{fig:rq3_privacy} sweep the per-round budget $\varepsilon \in \{0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, \infty\}$; the second column gives the composed end-to-end budget $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}} = T\varepsilon$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:dp} and its corollary). The mechanism is the calibrated per-class Laplace mechanism of Eq.~\eqref{eq:laplace}, whose noise scale $2R / (N_{c,k}\varepsilon)$ shrinks with the local class support $N_{c,k}$: prototypes over well-populated CWE buckets are naturally robust, while sparsely supported buckets receive proportionally more noise --- exactly the rows that the affinity weighting of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mcfpa} attenuates. All four metrics (F1, AUC, Precision, Recall) are reported at every budget.

\subsection{RQ4: Scalability}

Table~\ref{tab:rq4_scalability} and Figure~\ref{fig:rq4_scalability} vary $K \in \{3, 5, 10, 20\}$ over a fixed data pool (so larger $K$ means smaller, more fragmented clients). Communication is reported precisely: each client exchanges $2|\mathcal{C}|d$ float32 values per round ($10$\,KB in our configuration) regardless of $K$ or model size, and total server traffic grows linearly to $200$\,KB per round at $K = 20$ --- orders of magnitude below weight-sharing FL for typical code models (Section~\ref{sec:comm}).
